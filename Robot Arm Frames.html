2 · Coordinate system

Right-handed, Y up, and which way "positive" turns

OpenGL and GLM are right-handed: X × Y = Z. Your camera sits on the +Z side looking back at the arm, so on your screen X goes right, Y goes up and Z comes toward you. The arm grows along +Y and the base spins about Y.

The sign rule matters more than anything else on this page. Point your right thumb along an axis. Your fingers curl in the positive direction. Seen with that axis pointing at you, a positive angle turns counterclockwise. Seen with it pointing away from you, the same positive angle turns clockwise.

Fig 1 · Your camera's view of the axes, the two orthographic view keys, and the right-hand rule. So in the side view, where X points away, every positive Rx turns clockwise. In the top view, where Y points at you, a positive Ry turns counterclockwise.

5 · The frame chain

One rule, applied down the tree, rebuilt every frame

M_child = M_parent · T(offset) · R(angle) GLM: glm::rotate(glm::translate(parent, offset), angle, axis)

Each joint stores only where it sits relative to its parent and how far it has turned. Its world matrix comes from multiplying down the chain. Your program keeps five numbers between frames (baseDeg, shoulderDeg, elbowDeg, wristDeg, jawDeg) and rebuilds every matrix from glm::mat4(1.0f) each frame, so nothing accumulates.

Why T comes before R

T(d) · R_x(θ) = | 1 0 0 d_x | | 0 cosθ −sinθ d_y | | 0 sinθ cosθ d_z | | 0 0 0 1 |

The translation column stays exactly d whatever θ is, so the child pivots in place at the joint. Swap the order and the column becomes R_x(θ) · d: the offset itself rotates and the child orbits the parent's origin. This is Chapter 2's rotate-about-a-point idea T R T⁻¹; the trailing T⁻¹ isn't needed because each child is modelled starting at its own origin.

Why the gripper is mirrored

R_x(+θ) · (0, 1, 0) = (0, cos θ, +sin θ)

A positive Rx tips a finger toward +Z. For the upper jaw (side = +1, at z = +0.34) that is away from the centre: it opens. The lower jaw uses R_x(−jaw), which tips it toward −Z, also away from the centre. One loop over side = +1, −1 builds both jaws, and O opens them symmetrically.

6 · Unit shapes, normals and winding

Four generators, built once at unit size

Each shape is generated once in geometry.cpp and then sized and placed with matrices. The cylinder's base sits at the origin, not its centre, so T(0, length, 0) lands exactly on the next joint. Every vertex carries a position, a normal and a texture coordinate, as spec §2.2 requires.

Fig 5 · Red arrows are the normals your generators store. Flat faces (caps, disc, box faces) get one normal per face and their own copies of every corner vertex. Curved surfaces (cylinder wall, sphere) get smooth radial normals. The spec writes the wall normal as (cos θ, sin θ, 0) because its example cylinder runs along Z; yours runs along Y, so (cos θ, 0, sin θ) is the same thing.

Winding, checked by cross product

OpenGL treats a triangle as front-facing when its corners run counterclockwise seen from outside, and you enabled GL_CULL_FACE, so any inward-wound triangle disappears. The face direction comes from (v₁ − v₀) × (v₂ − v₀). With Δθ = 360°/48 = 7.5°, rim₁ = (0.991, 0, 0.131):

The 3D views on this page also run with back-face culling on. If any of these were wrong, a cap would vanish there too.

Why normals need their own matrix

fragmentNormal = mat3(transpose(inverse(view · model))) · normal

Your links are stretched unevenly, for example S(0.22, 2.80, 0.22). Multiplying a normal by that same matrix would tilt it off the surface. The inverse-transpose keeps it perpendicular. mat3(…) drops the translation column, because moving a surface does not change which way it faces. The fragment shader then calls normalize, because interpolation across a triangle shortens the vector.

7 · Texture coordinates

How your two PNGs land on the arm

A texture coordinate (u, v) is an address inside the image: (0, 0) is one corner, (1, 1) the opposite one. It says nothing about where the vertex is in space. Your Mesh flips each image vertically before upload, so v = 0 is the bottom row of the PNG. Values above 1 wrap around because the texture uses GL_REPEAT.

Fig 6 · makeCylinder(48, 4.0f, 2.0f) multiplies u by 4 and v by 2 on the wall, so the PNG tiles 4 times around and twice along. Both walls are drawn at the same scale (1 unit = 70 px), with your real texture. The caps keep the plain disc UVs.

11 · Lighting and materials

Per-fragment Phong, one point light, three materials

Your fragment shader works in view space, where the camera is at the origin. That is why normalize(−fragmentPosition) is the direction to the eye, and why the light position is moved into view space once on the CPU (view · lightPositionWorld) before it goes to the shader.

N = normalize(fragmentNormal) L = normalize(lightPositionView − fragmentPosition) V = normalize(−fragmentPosition) diffuse = max(N · L, 0) specular = diffuse > 0 ? max(V · reflect(−L, N), 0) ^ shininess : 0 color = baseColor · (ambient + diffuseK · diffuse) + specularK · specular baseColor = texture texel, or white

The texture scales the ambient and diffuse terms, as spec §2.4 asks. Specular stays white, so highlights look like the material and not the paint. The diffuse > 0 check stops a highlight appearing on a surface that faces away from the light.
